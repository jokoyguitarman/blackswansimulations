Key highlights

Four things that make it behave like a real crisis

These are the design decisions that separate an exercise here from a scripted tabletop, and the reason your team cannot settle into a comfortable answer.

01

Multi-agency coordination

Crises do not respect a single jurisdiction, and operational success usually depends on coordinated action across several. Every participating agency works inside one shared environment, entering decisions concurrently rather than waiting its turn. The pressure lands where multi-agency response actually fails: asset deployment across organisations, communication workflows, and joint command.

02

Information asymmetry

No agency ever holds a complete assessment of the theatre, so nobody is given one. Information is deliberately partitioned into role-specific intelligence streams, stripping out the artificial clarity that makes conventional exercises easy. What is left is the real bottleneck: whether teams can build the protocols to validate each other’s information quickly enough to construct a shared operational picture.

03

Real-time adaptive injects

There is no storyboard to work through. A condition-dependent engine evaluates what participants actually do and branches the scenario in real time, so the incident escalates or settles according to the speed and quality of the response. That removes predictability entirely — the trajectory follows the decisions, not a script.

04

Team interdependency and storyboard synthesis

Operational domains are interdependent, so no decision is made in isolation. One team’s intervention alters the conditions every other team is working in, because a central synthesis layer feeds the output of each module straight back in as live input to the others. Separate operational threads run as a single engine, and teams have to absorb the compounding friction their counterparts create.

The readiness gap

The readiness gap

“No plan of operations extends with any certainty beyond the first contact with the main body of the enemy.”

Helmuth von Moltke the Elder

Almost every organisation has a crisis plan. The readiness gap appears when people must execute it under time pressure, incomplete information and changing conditions.

Decisions arrive late

The correct call, made twenty minutes too late, is a different call. Nobody measures this in a discussion-based exercise.

Information stops at the function boundary

Operations knew. Communications did not. In the real event, that gap becomes the story.

The first hour is lost to others

Speculation and misinformation set the narrative while your holding statement is still circulating for approval.

Nobody is certain who decides

Escalation thresholds and delegated authority look unambiguous on paper and dissolve under time pressure.

Untested assumptions fail quietly

The plan assumes a system, a supplier or a named individual that turns out to be unavailable on the day.

And none of it is measured

A conventional tabletop ends in a discussion and a subjective debrief. You cannot track something you never measured, which is the gap we close.

Exercise types

Three ways to put your team under pressure

Same scoring and the same report in every case. We recommend a format once we understand what you are trying to prove.

Type 01

Multi-agency coordination

Tests the handoffs — between your own functions, and with the external agencies you would actually depend on. Each team sees a different slice of the truth.

  • Deliberate information asymmetry between teams
  • Role-scoped channels, resource requests and negotiation
  • Best suited to command groups and executive teams

Type 02

Site and public safety response

For organisations responsible for physical sites and the people in them. A live map of a real location, with a crowd simulation running underneath that reacts to every decision.

  • Evacuation and cordon decisions with modelled consequences
  • Casualty condition deteriorates while decisions are pending
  • Built on real building footprints and street layouts

Type 03

Reputational and social crisis

For communications, legal and leadership. A simulated feed, inbox and news cycle turns on your organisation while misinformation moves faster than your approval chain.

  • Simulated posts, direct messages and news coverage
  • Every public statement is fact-checked against the scenario
  • Hostile actors that respond to what you actually say

Practicalities

What running one actually involves

The honest answer to the questions procurement and your chief of staff will ask first.

Time
Around half a day per live simulation, including the briefing and the immediate debrief. The two simulations usually sit a few weeks apart.
People
Eight to thirty participants across three to five teams. Bring the people who would genuinely be in the room, not their delegates.
Technology
A browser. Nothing for participants to install, and voice communications are built in rather than bolted on.
Location
One room, several sites, or fully remote. Separating the teams usually makes the exercise harder in a useful way, because they can no longer read each other.
From you
One scoping call and whatever incident material you already have — a plan, a risk register, an SOP. We build the rest and you approve it.
Facilitation
A Black Swan risk consultant designs the scenario with you, facilitates both live simulations, and presents the scored after-action review to your leadership.

Safety

Safe to rehearse, and safe to hand to us

Fictional and non-operational

Scenarios are invented. They are built to resemble your exposure closely enough to be uncomfortable, without documenting a real target, a real vulnerability or a real operational plan.

Nothing touches a real platform

The feeds, inboxes, news outlets and messaging your people use during the exercise are all simulated inside the environment. Nothing a participant posts can reach the public internet or a real system.

Data handling agreed up front

Exactly what material you share, how it is processed while the scenario is built, and what is retained afterwards is settled during scoping — before anything is created. Bring your security team to that call.

Questions

Before you take this to your leadership

How is this different from the tabletop exercise we already run?

A tabletop is usually a structured discussion following a fixed script, closed out by a subjective debrief. Here the situation advances on a clock and reacts to what your team does or fails to do, so late decisions have consequences you can see. The debrief is then assembled from measured decisions rather than from what people remember agreeing.

Who designs and runs it?

A Black Swan risk consultant. They build the scenario with you from your own material, facilitate both simulations, and present the scored after-action review. You are not being handed software to operate yourself.

Do our people need training on the system first?

No. Participants receive a role briefing and work in a browser. The interface is deliberately plain, because the exercise is testing judgement and coordination, not anyone’s proficiency with a tool.

Can you build it around our existing crisis plan?

That is the preferred input. The exercise is most valuable when it tests the plan you would genuinely follow, including the assumptions inside it that have never been put under load.

How long until the first simulation?

Most of the elapsed time is your review, not our build. Once we have your material a draft scenario comes back quickly, and we then iterate until you are satisfied it reflects your real exposure. We will give you a firm date at the scoping call.

What exactly do we hold at the end of it?

A scored after-action review covering both simulations: a narrative of what happened, metrics per team and participant, the complete decision timeline, and prioritised recommendations. Exported to PDF or Excel for a board or audit pack.

What does an engagement cost?

We go through it at the scoping call, once we know which format you need and how many people are taking part. An engagement is quoted as a single figure covering the scenario, both live simulations and the scored after-action review, so there is nothing to reconcile afterwards.
