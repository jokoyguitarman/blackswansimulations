Replay section — concepts

Turning the timeline into a storyboard that actually applies pressure

Why the current version is inert

  • All six events sit on screen at once. The ending is readable in the same glance as the opening, so there is no suspense left to build.
  • Every row is the same size. The worst moment of the exercise is visually row four of six.
  • Each row says what happened, never what it cost. The reader is left to supply the consequence themselves, and mostly will not bother.
  • The numbers never move. Latency and coordination are fixed text; only their opacity pulses.
  • The escalation meter is on its own timer. It is not synchronised to the event that supposedly caused it.
  • It looks like a server log. Monospace timestamps in uniform rows is the aesthetic of monitoring, not of crisis.

What creates the tension instead

  • One board at a time. You cannot see what is coming, which is the entire feeling being sold.
  • Every board names the cost. Not “footage circulating” but “the public is now ahead of your command group”.
  • Numbers move as the board lands. Mentions count up, trust counts down, in front of the reader.
  • The panel changes temperature. Calm, then amber, then a red frame with a slow sweep, then recovery.
  • It ends on the product. The last board is what the report will say, which is the thing being bought.
  • One board is inherently mobile. A single panel needs no reflow, unlike a two-column dashboard.

Concept 1 — recommended

Auto-advancing storyboard

Plays itself when it scrolls into view, one board replacing the last. The reader can take over at any point using the rail, and it pauses while they hover or focus it.

The incident is confirmed at your primary site.

Operations know. Nobody outside that room does. This is the only moment in the next hour when your organisation is ahead of the story, and it lasts nine minutes.

T+00:15

Spreading

Footage is public before your first statement is drafted.

Someone in the car park filmed the evacuation. The public now knows more than your own communications team, and every minute from here is spent catching up rather than leading.

T+00:22

Escalating

The regulator wants a briefing within the hour.

Legal advises saying nothing until the cause is confirmed. Communications argues the silence is already the story. Both are partly right, and in the exercise nobody is certain whose call it actually is.

T+00:31

Out of control

Still no holding statement.

An ex-employee’s claim that an internal audit flagged this months ago is now the most-shared version of events. It is unverified, and it is winning. The story has stopped being about the incident and started being about the silence.

T+00:47

Correcting

Communications issues a corrected statement.

It is accurate, careful and signed off. It reaches a small fraction of the people who saw the claim it was written to answer, because corrections never travel as far as the thing they correct.

T+01:04

Contained

Narrative stabilised. Situation contained.

The team recovered it. But the after-action review is not about the recovery, it is about the twenty-two minutes of silence that made the recovery necessary — timed to the second, attributed to a decision point, and sitting in a report you can hand to your board.

Illustrative replay of a representative exercise

Mobile behaviour

Nothing reflows. One board is already one column, so the phone gets the same sequence with a taller stage and a three-up metric strip that stays on one line.

If the script fails

All six boards are in the HTML and simply read as a vertical list, so the story is still there for crawlers and for anyone the script never reaches.

Cost

Around sixty lines of vanilla JS in the existing marketing bundle. No library, no images, and it starts on view rather than on load.

Concept 2

Scroll-driven storyboard

The same boards, but the reader’s scroll is the clock. The panel holds still while the crisis advances under it. Scroll down slowly to feel the difference.

Pressure 14

Trade-off

Thematically this is the better fit — the reader’s own scrolling becomes the clock running out, which is exactly the feeling. The cost is that it consumes roughly three screens of scroll depth, pushing the rest of the page down, and sticky positioning is the first thing to misbehave on older mobile browsers. I would ship Concept 1 now and keep this in reserve.

Recommendation

Concept 1. It gives you the fade-to-replace storyboard you described, it is genuinely better on a phone than the current two-column dashboard, and it degrades to a readable list if the script never runs. The single change doing most of the work is not the animation at all — it is that every board now names the consequence, and the last one hands the reader the report they would be buying.
