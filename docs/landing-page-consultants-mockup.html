Key highlights

Four things that make it behave like a real crisis

The design principles behind every scenario you build, and the reason an exercise here puts far more pressure on a team than a paper tabletop can.

01

Multi-agency coordination

Crises do not respect a single jurisdiction, and operational success usually depends on coordinated action across several. Every participating agency works inside one shared environment, entering decisions concurrently rather than waiting its turn. The pressure lands where multi-agency response actually fails: asset deployment across organisations, communication workflows, and joint command.

02

Information asymmetry

No agency ever holds a complete assessment of the theatre, so nobody is given one. Information is deliberately partitioned into role-specific intelligence streams, stripping out the artificial clarity that makes conventional exercises easy. What is left is the real bottleneck: whether teams can build the protocols to validate each other’s information quickly enough to construct a shared operational picture.

03

Real-time adaptive injects

There is no storyboard to work through. A condition-dependent engine evaluates what participants actually do and branches the scenario in real time, so the incident escalates or settles according to the speed and quality of the response. That removes predictability entirely — the trajectory follows the decisions, not a script.

04

Team interdependency and storyboard synthesis

Operational domains are interdependent, so no decision is made in isolation. One team’s intervention alters the conditions every other team is working in, because a central synthesis layer feeds the output of each module straight back in as live input to the others. Separate operational threads run as a single engine, and teams have to absorb the compounding friction their counterparts create.

How it works

Four steps, start to signed-off report

  1. 01

    Bring your brief

    Upload the incident brief, doctrine or SOP you already work from. The blueprint engine drafts the scenario, the NPCs, the fact sheets and each team’s storyline. You review and edit every line.

  2. 02

    Invite the teams

    Send one join link. Participants sign in through a browser, read their role briefing and wait in the lobby. Nothing for them to install.

  3. 03

    Run it live

    Injects fire on schedule or when conditions trigger. Adversary NPCs push back, misinformation spreads, the clock runs at the speed you set. You observe, nudge and take notes.

  4. 04

    Hand over the AAR

    The after-action review is written for you: narrative summary, per-team metrics and recommendations. Export it, then walk your client through the timeline replay.

Producing the AAR also closes out the engagement and queues your payout.

Exercise types

Three very different rooms to put a team in

Same platform, same scoring, same report. Pick the format that fits the objective.

Format 01

Multi-agency coordination

A shared common operating picture where police, medical, comms and command see different slices of the truth. The exercise is about information flow, chain of command and who tells whom.

  • Deliberate information blind spots between teams
  • Role-scoped channels and resource negotiation
  • Best for executive and command-level audiences

Format 02

Mass-casualty field response

A live map of a real location with a crowd simulation running underneath it. Move units, set cordons, open evacuation corridors — and watch the crowd actually respond to what you did.

  • Physics-driven crowd evacuation and casualty deterioration
  • Fog of war, hazards and unit-level vision
  • Built on real building footprints and street layouts

Format 03

Social media crisis war room

A simulated feed, inbox and news cycle that turns on your client. Comms, legal, procurement and sales fight a reputational fire while misinformation outruns them.

  • Simulated posts, DMs, groups and news coverage
  • AI-generated images and video in the feed
  • Hostile actors coordinating against your statements

Questions

What consultants ask first

Do I have to write scenarios from scratch?

No. Bring the material you already use — an incident brief, a doctrine document, a client SOP — and the platform drafts the scenario, the teams, the NPCs and the inject schedule from it. You then edit anything you disagree with. The point is to skip the blank page, not to take your expertise out of it.

What do my participants need to install?

Nothing. They open a join link in a browser, on a laptop or a phone. Voice comms run in the browser too, so there is no separate conference bridge to manage.

Can I run the exercise remotely, or does everyone need to be in one room?

Either works. Everything is real-time over the web, so teams can sit in separate rooms, separate buildings or separate countries. Splitting teams up often makes the exercise harder in a useful way, because they can no longer read each other across the table.

Why two simulations per engagement?

So you can show movement. Run the scenario before your training to establish a baseline, teach, then run it again afterwards. Because outcomes branch on what the team actually does, the second run is a genuine test rather than a memory exercise — and the score difference is the strongest thing you can put in front of a client.

When exactly do I get paid?

Your share is set aside as soon as your client’s invoice is settled, and it is queued for release the moment you produce the after-action review. Release is typically within two business days, straight to the bank account you verified during payout setup. The commercial terms are shared with you when you join.

Is any of this connected to real operations?

No. Scenarios are fictional and non-operational by design, and the simulated feeds, inboxes and news outlets exist only inside the exercise. Nothing your participants post can reach a real platform, which is what makes it safe to hand to a sensitive client.
