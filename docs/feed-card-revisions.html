Hero card — revisions

Making the public feed card read like a feed — and land like a crisis

Why the current card fails

  • Grey skeleton bars where names go. That is the visual language of a loading state, so the eye reads “unfinished wireframe”, not “social platform”.
  • No engagement row. Reply, repost, like and view icons are the single most recognisable signature of a feed. Without them it is just stacked boxes.
  • No handles, no timestamps, no verification. Nothing anchors it to a real platform.
  • The copy is commentary about a crisis, not the crisis. “Still no statement. That tells you something” is a shrug. Nobody’s pulse moves.
  • Nothing is at stake for the reader. No deadline, no insider, no journalist, no consequence. So there is nothing to project themselves into.

What makes it land instead

  • Use the real product’s chrome. The in-app network is branded Z, and posts really do carry a 0–100 virality score, misinformation flags and a response deadline. Showing those makes the card a screenshot rather than an illustration.
  • Ordinary words, not dramatic ones. “They’re walking everyone out and she isn’t picking up” is frightening. “A crisis is unfolding” is not.
  • Cast the three voices every comms lead dreads: the bystander with a photo, the ex-insider claiming documents exist, and the reporter with a filing time.
  • Show the asymmetry. The false claim outrunning the truth by two orders of magnitude is the whole argument for rehearsal, in one glance.
  • Put a clock on it. A visible countdown converts a picture into pressure.

All three use a fictional organisation and are tagged as exercise material, matching the disclaimer already on the page. Counters and clocks below are live so you can see how they behave in the hero.

Revision 1

The ladder

The story climbs through three voices in ninety seconds: a frightened bystander, an ex-employee claiming documents, then national media with a filing deadline. Ends on a clock, so the card asks the reader a question rather than telling them a story.

Revision 2

The silence

Makes the reader’s own account the subject. Their last post is a cheerful award announcement from Tuesday, still pinned at the top while the replies turn. This is the one that makes a communications lead wince, because they have seen it happen to someone else.

Revision 3

The lie is winning

One comparison, no reading required. The false claim and the careful official statement sit one above the other with their real numbers showing. The gap between them is the entire argument for rehearsing, and it lands in about two seconds.

My recommendation

Revision 1 for the hero. It is the only one of the three that works as a tall card, which is the shape the fan needs, and it carries the most product truth per square inch: three different NPC archetypes, a live virality score, a misinformation flag and a response clock. It also ends on a question rather than a conclusion, which suits a hero.

Revision 3 further down the page, where there is room for a wide two-up and where a single hard number does more work than a narrative. Revision 2 is the strongest emotionally but it only bites once the reader already understands the product, so it belongs in the section about scoring rather than at the top.
